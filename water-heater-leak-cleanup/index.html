Water Heater Leak Cleanup in Reston: What to Know

Even though the heater itself contains 40 to 80 gallons, the leak is not capped at that amount. Because the supply line feeds the tank, a rusted out bottom drains and refills at the same pace. It will run until the cold water valve gets closed. The location of the heater sets the damage. It may be a hallway closet, a garage floor or, the worst case, an attic over finished rooms.

In Reston, we start with water extraction and then inspect everything around the heater. The platform beneath the tank and the base of the closet walls are usually soaked. When the heater is in the garage, items kept on the slab get wet and so does the wall adjoining the house. From an attic it comes down through insulation and ceilings. We measure each area and dry it with air movers and dehumidifiers.

First Five Minutes

Your First Steps

  1. Turn Off the Heat SourceElectric units get shut down at the breaker. For gas, turn the control knob to off. Running the burner or the element with no water in the tank is something to avoid every time.
  2. Shut the Cold Water ValveThe cold line over the tank has a valve, and it closes with a clockwise turn. If it is stuck or leaking, shut off the main water valve for the house.
  3. Watch for Hot WaterTank water is hot enough to scald. Keep children and pets away, and do not wade through pooled water until it has had time to cool.
  4. Empty the Tank OutsideIf you can reach the drain valve, attach a garden hose and run it outside or to a floor drain so the remaining water does not empty onto the floor.
  5. Clear the FloorAnything absorbent, including boxes and rugs, should come up from the wet area. Fabric and cardboard absorb water fast, then keep it pressed to the concrete and walls.

Why It Happens

How It Usually Starts

  • Rust at the connections above the tank, either the flex lines or the inlet and outlet fittings.
  • Fittings left slightly loose by recent repair or installation work.
  • Anode rods that are used up, leaving the steel inside the tank open to rust.
  • The relief valve for temperature and pressure opening, or failing, and letting water out.
  • An aging steel tank whose bottom is eaten through by corrosion and sediment.
Why Speed Matters

What the Water Damages

Ceilings Under an Attic Heater

Water from an attic unit runs down through insulation to the ceiling beneath it. Wet insulation stops insulating and its weight can bring ceiling drywall down.

Platform and Subfloor

More water goes into the subfloor or wooden stand beneath the tank than anywhere else. That wood may rot and lose strength, and it will have to carry the weight of a new tank filled with water.

Shared Garage Wall

Water crossing a garage slab soaks the bottom plate and drywall of the wall shared with the house, then moves into the flooring on the living side.

Adjacent Room Flooring

From a heater closet, the leak slips beneath the door and reaches hallway hardwood, laminate or carpet. Heat makes it worse, with quicker swelling of wood boards and carpet backing that comes apart.

Coverage

Does Insurance Cover It?

Water damage to belongings, walls and floors from a tank that fails suddenly is often covered as a sudden and accidental loss. Usually the unit is left out of the claim, as age related wear is viewed as a maintenance matter. If an adjuster finds signs of a long-term leak, such as rust trails or old staining, the claim can be reduced or denied because gradual leaks usually are not covered. Read your policy, take photos of the damage and the heater, and do not dispose of the old unit before the claim is settled.

Pricing

What Drives the Price

  • How much insulation, ceiling or wall drywall needs to be taken out.
  • Placement of the unit, as leaks from an attic or an inside closet run higher than those in a garage.
  • Amount of contents affected, since wet belongings have to be relocated, dried or discarded.
  • Any rebuilding of the subfloor or platform beneath the tank ahead of the new heater.
